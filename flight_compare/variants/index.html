Experimental scattering variants

Ni et al. (1999) section V states that the model has been validated over dense forests, and defers three things that matter as a canopy opens up: the bidirectional effect of the background, the exchange of radiation between background and canopy, and scattering between neighbouring crowns. The -rt_* options switch them on one at a time, and each page below is that option measured against the same FLIGHT Monte Carlo.

None of them is on by default. With no -rt_* option gortt is the paper as published, and all 82 original regression goldens are byte-identical — that is the guard. FLIGHT was not re-run for any of this.

What they are worth

Bias and RMSD against FLIGHT as a percentage of FLIGHT's own mean, over all 12 scenes × 3 solar zeniths × 64 directions. The red band is the guard: it already agrees, and a formulation that improves the near infrared at its expense is not an improvement.

  • crown-path is the one that works. It takes the near-infrared bias from −7.4% to +1.7% and lowers the RMSD as well, which a pure rescaling would not do — it is fixing a shape error, not adding a constant. In the sparse scenes, where the published approximation is furthest outside the range section V claims, it goes from −9.5% to +2.5% and the RMSD falls from 18.4% to 13.9%. The red band moves by 0.8 points, i.e. not at all.
  • It is also near-inert where the paper was validated. On the paper's own SOBS canopy it moves the Fig. 7b sunlit-crown signature from 0.470 to 0.474. That is the property to want: it should change the sparse canopies and leave the dense ones alone.
  • squeeze-path is the better answer for sparse canopies and the worse one overall (sparse RMSD 13.3% against 13.9%, but +6.6% bias overall against +1.7%). The two bracket the truth rather than settling it, which is honest: one spreads the foliage over the crowns' own footprint, the other over the part of the hemisphere that is not open, and nothing here says which a crown really is.
  • The recollision probability does not earn its place, and the reason is measurable. run_flight_single.py --black-soil measures p from FLIGHT directly: with a black soil the canopy alone satisfies BRF(all orders)/BRF(single) = 1/(1−ωp). Against gortt's Wigner estimate p = σl/(1+σl) on the crown's mean chord: 0.435 against 0.37–0.46 for the thin crowns (good), but 0.909 against 0.53–0.57 for the thick ones. The first-flight escape probability assumes the scattering is uniformly distributed through the crown; in an optically thick crown it happens near the lit surface, where escape is easier, so p comes out too high. Correct in the thin limit, wrong where it matters.
  • bg-coupling is small, safe and in the right direction — −7.4% to −4.0% with the RMSD very slightly better in both bands. It is the least arguable of the five: as it stands the sunlit background cannot see the canopy at all.
  • crown-crown is not supported. It improves the sparse bias and wrecks the RMSD (18.6% to 37.4% overall), i.e. it adds brightness in the wrong places. The paper calls it "usually small and very complicated to model"; on this evidence the first-order form here is not the way to put it back.
  • They do not compose. all overshoots by +43.4%. crown-path and recollision are two answers to the same question, and adding crown-crown on top counts the same photons a third time.

The lidar is untouched by all of this and is not repeated per variant: gortt_lidar takes its backscatter coefficients straight from the leaf transmittance and the soil albedo and never calls gortt_rt_init. Checked, not assumed — gortt -lidar -rt_all is byte-identical to gortt -lidar.
